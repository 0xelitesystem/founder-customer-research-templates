# 01. Discovery interview

## Goal

Find out whether the person has a real, frequent, expensive problem that they are actively trying to solve. Not whether they want your product. You should not describe your product in this call.

Walk away with:
- A specific, recent story of them dealing with the problem
- The current workaround (this is your competition)
- How much time, money, or stress the problem costs
- Whether they searched for a solution recently

If you can't get those four things, the problem is not real enough to build for.

## Setup

> "Thanks for hopping on. I'm doing some research into [problem area], and I'm trying to understand how people deal with it today. I'm not selling anything and I'm going to ask you questions about your own work, not pitch you on anything. Sound good? Mind if I record this so I don't miss anything?"

Keep this to 30 seconds. Don't explain what you're building. Don't even hint.

## Script

### Part 1: Their context (3-5 minutes)

1. Walk me through what you do in a typical week. What takes up most of your time?
2. Where does [problem area] fit into that? How does it come up?
3. Who else on your team or in your life touches this?

### Part 2: A recent specific instance (10-15 minutes, the meat of the call)

4. Think about the most recent time you dealt with [problem]. When was it?
5. Walk me through what happened. Start with what triggered it.
6. (After their first answer) What did you do next? And then?
7. How long did it take? Was that typical?
8. Who else was involved?
9. What did you end up using to handle it? Spreadsheet? Tool? Email thread?
10. How did it end? Was it actually resolved?

For every "I usually..." answer, ask: "Can you tell me about the most recent time?". Generalizations are useless. You need one specific story with timestamps.

### Part 3: Workarounds and friction (5-7 minutes)

11. What part of that process annoyed you the most?
12. Did you ever try a tool or system to make it easier? What happened?
13. How did you find that tool? What made you stop using it (if you did)?
14. If you could wave a wand and fix one thing about how this works today, what would it be?

### Part 4: Spend and search (3-5 minutes)

15. Has your team ever paid for anything to help with this? What?
16. (If yes) How much? How was the decision made?
17. (If no) What would have to be true for you to consider paying?
18. Have you searched for a tool to help with this in the last 6 months? What did you search for?

### Wrap-up

19. Who else should I talk to about this?
20. Is it okay if I follow up in a few weeks if I have more questions?

## Anti-patterns

These questions look reasonable but produce garbage data. Avoid them.

| Bad question | Why it's bad | Use this instead |
|--------------|--------------|------------------|
| "Would you use a product that does X?" | Hypothetical. They'll say yes to be polite. | "Tell me about the last time you tried to do X." |
| "How often do you do X?" | Forces them to estimate. They'll guess. | "When was the last time you did X?" |
| "What features would you want?" | They're not the product designer. They don't know. | "What was the most frustrating part of doing it today?" |
| "Does this sound useful?" | Yes-trap. They'll agree. | (Don't describe your product.) |
| "Is price an issue for you?" | They'll say yes to get a discount. | "What does your team currently pay for tools in this space?" |
| "Do you have a budget for this?" | Same. | "How did the last tool purchase in this category get approved?" |

## After the call

Within an hour, write down:
- The specific story they told (timestamps, names, tools, dollars)
- The exact wording they used for the problem (this becomes your landing page copy)
- One thing that surprised you
- What you'd ask differently next time

If you talked more than 30 percent of the call, you talked too much.
